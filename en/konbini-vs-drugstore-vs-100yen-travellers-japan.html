Konbini vs drugstore vs 100-yen shop in Japan — where should a tourist actually buy?

🧳 For visitors buying snacks, drinks & essentials in Japan
⏱ 30-second answer
  • Konbini (7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart) is everywhere and open 24/7 — but for snacks, drinks and toiletries it's usually the most expensive option.
  • Drugstores (Matsumoto Kiyoshi, Welcia, Sugi, etc.) are typically the cheapest for drinks, snacks, medicine and cosmetics — and many are tax-free for tourists over the minimum spend. See how tax-free works →
  • 100-yen shops (Daiso) win on cheap travel gear; supermarkets win on food, especially half-price in the evening.

Every tourist ends up in a konbini within an hour of landing — it's convenient, it's bright, it's open. But convenience is a product you're paying for. Mio doesn't care that it's easy; here's where the same items actually cost less.

Konbini: convenient, but you pay for it

A convenience store (konbini) is unbeatable for one thing: it's open 24/7 and it's everywhere. It's genuinely the right choice for an ATM that takes foreign cards, buying event/transport tickets, printing, parcel drop-off, or a hot meal at 2am. But for the everyday basket — a bottle of water, a bag of snacks, travel-size toiletries — you're typically paying a convenience premium of roughly 20–50% over a drugstore (illustrative, varies by item). If you buy your daily drinks and snacks here for a week, that premium quietly adds up.

Drugstores: the traveller's cheat code

Japanese drugstores (Matsukiyo / Matsumoto Kiyoshi, Welcia, Sugi, Cosmos, and others) are far more than pharmacies. They stock drinks, snacks, instant food, cosmetics, sunscreen and over-the-counter medicine — usually at the lowest everyday prices of the three. The tourist bonus: many drugstores offer tax-free shopping once you pass the minimum purchase (generally ¥5,000+ in one store, same day) with your passport. That means buying toiletries and consumables in bulk at a drugstore can beat konbini twice — lower shelf price and no consumption tax.

Effective saving ≈ (konbini price − drugstore price) + tax-free discount on the drugstore basketIllustrative. Tax-free rules, minimum spend and eligible items are set by law and by each store — always confirm at the counter.

100-yen shops: cheap travel gear, not food

Daiso and other 100-yen shops (hyaku-en shop) are where you fix travel problems cheaply: travel adapters, umbrellas, phone cables, packing containers, snacks, and souvenirs for about ¥100–300 each. They're not the cheapest place for a week of groceries, but for the "I forgot to pack X" moment they beat a konbini or department store by a wide margin. Note prices are usually shown before tax, so a ¥100 item rings up a little higher.

Supermarkets: the cheapest actual food

If you have kitchen access or just want a cheaper lunch, a supermarket (super) beats all of the above on food. The traveller's trick: many supermarkets discount fresh bento, sushi and deli items with half-price stickers in the evening (often after ~7–8pm) as they clear that day's stock. A dinner that would cost ¥800 at a konbini can be ¥300–400 at a supermarket near closing time — illustrative, but the pattern is real.

Mio's rule

Use the konbini only for what it's uniquely good at — convenience, ATM withdrawals, tickets, and late-night emergencies. Buy drinks, snacks and toiletries at a drugstore (in bulk, and tax-free with your passport if you clear the minimum). Buy travel gear at a 100-yen shop, and buy meals at a supermarket, ideally in the evening. The item is the same; only the meter changes with the door you walk through.

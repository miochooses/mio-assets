Japan's hotel tax (宿泊税): the per-night charge that isn't in your booking price

🧳 For visitors who booked a hotel online and don't want a surprise cash charge at check-in — especially families, groups, and anyone staying in Kyoto.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • Big Japanese cities charge an accommodation tax (宿泊税) that is usually not included in the price you paid online. It's per person, per night, on top of the 10% consumption tax, and often collected at the hotel at check-in or check-out.
  • For most budget/mid-range stays it's small — roughly ¥100–¥500 per person per night. But it stacks: a group or a long stay turns "small" into real money.
  • Kyoto is the outlier. From 1 March 2026 its tax runs up to ¥10,000 per person per night on the most expensive rooms, and no stay is exempt — even the cheapest room is taxed ¥200. Where you sleep is a money decision →

You compared hotels, found a good price, paid online — done, right? Not quite. Several Japanese cities levy a separate accommodation tax (shukuhaku-zei, 宿泊税) that many booking sites don't fold into the headline price. It's typically added per guest, per night, and in a lot of hotels you settle it at the front desk — sometimes in cash. It's rarely huge, but it's the kind of non-obvious charge that catches travellers off guard, and in Kyoto from 2026 it's genuinely significant.

How it works (the part that trips people up)

Per person, per night. The tax is charged for each guest for each night — so two people for five nights pay it ten times, not once. Separate from the room price. It sits on top of the nightly rate and the 10% consumption tax, and it's frequently not shown in the total you saw online. Collected by the hotel, not the booking site. Many properties add it to your bill or ask for it at check-in, so budget a little cash even if you prepaid the room.

Rates are per guest per night on the room rate (excluding consumption tax). Kyoto has no exemption — every stay is taxed at least ¥200. Tokyo has announced a move to a percentage-based tax (around 3% of the room rate) in a future year; check the current rule before a high-end Tokyo stay.

Not a trip-killer — but it's real, it's on top of everything else, and at a ¥100,000+ Kyoto suite the tax is ¥10,000 per person, per night, which absolutely is.

Mio's verdict

Budget it, don't fear it. For ordinary stays the accommodation tax is a rounding error — but a rounding error that's usually missing from the price you compared, so add ¥100–¥500 per person per night when you tally your real hotel cost, and keep a little cash for check-in.

Kyoto is where it becomes strategy. Its 2026 top tiers are far above any other city, and there's no exemption. If you're eyeing a pricey Kyoto room, one clean money move is to stay in Osaka (lower tax, cheaper rooms, and Kyoto is a short train ride) and day-trip in — you dodge both the higher Kyoto room rate and its steeper tax. The tax won't decide your trip, but knowing it exists keeps it from deciding your budget for you.

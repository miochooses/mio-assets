Is Japanese tap water actually safe?

Short version: yes, per Japan's waterworks standards. Tap water in Japan is treated and tested against a legally-regulated set of drinking-water quality standards, and it is safe to drink in almost all of the country. What does vary is taste and hardness by region — most of Japan is soft water, and some areas taste better than others. If you dislike the taste, that's a flavour problem, not a safety problem — and it's the exact problem a filter jug solves cheaply.

The three options, side by side

Tap costs a few tenths of a yen per litre — effectively free at the point of drinking. A filter jug (Brita-style) adds a small amount per litre for replacement cartridges, mainly to improve taste. Bottled water from a supermarket or convenience store is the expensive option — you're paying for packaging, transport, and the store margin, not for safer water.

The trap most people miss: paying for safety you already have

Many people buy bottled water assuming it's safer than the tap. In Japan that assumption usually doesn't hold — the tap already meets the drinking-water standard. So the bottle mostly buys you convenience and taste, at 100–500× the price. If taste is the real issue, a filter jug or a filtered pitcher gives you most of the taste benefit for a tiny fraction of the bottled cost.

Smart middle path: drink tap for cooking and most glasses; keep a filter jug in the fridge for cold, better-tasting drinking water; buy the occasional bottle only when you're out and about — not as your default supply.

Mio's verdict

Drink the tap — it's safe per Japan's waterworks standards and costs almost nothing. Add a filter jug if you care about taste. Reserve bottled for convenience only. The "right" answer isn't about what feels cleaner — it's about not paying 100–500× per litre for water you already have at the sink.

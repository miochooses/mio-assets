Suica/PASMO vs cash on Japan's trains — and when a commuter pass (teiki) actually pays off

🚃 For anyone living in Japan who commutes by train and wonders whether to tap an IC card, pay cash, or buy a commuter pass.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • IC card (Suica/PASMO) beats cash almost always: the IC fare is usually a few yen cheaper per ride than a paper ticket, and tapping is faster. Put Suica/PASMO on your phone and you never queue at a machine.
  • The real decision is the commuter pass (定期券, teiki). Rough rule: if you make the same trip ~15+ round trips a month, a teiki usually beats pay-as-you-go — and it covers any trip inside its route for free.
  • Buy the 6-month teiki if you can — it's cheaper per month than the 1-month. Students get commuter discounts. Note the ¥500 IC deposit is refundable. See the full arrival checklist →

Trains are the one bill a commuter in Japan pays almost every day, so a small per-ride difference compounds fast. Mio doesn't care whether tapping "feels" cheaper — here's the math.

IC card vs paper ticket vs cash

A rechargeable IC card — Suica, PASMO, and their regional siblings — is the default. Two reasons: the IC fare is usually a few yen lower than the equivalent paper ticket on many operators, and tapping through the gate is faster than buying a ticket each time. Over 40+ rides a month, a few yen each adds up, and the time saved is free.

Mobile Suica / Mobile PASMO (in Apple Wallet or on Android) go one step further: no plastic card, no ticket machine, and you can top up from your phone. If you have a smartphone, there's little reason to carry a physical card except the refundable deposit sitting on it.

Cash/paper ticket ≈ IC fare + a few yen, every single rideIllustrative. The exact gap varies by operator and route — check your line. IC is faster either way.

The real question: teiki (commuter pass) vs pay-as-you-go

A teiki is a fixed-price pass for one specific route (station A ↔ station B) over 1, 3, or 6 months. Inside that route it's effectively unlimited: you can ride the same segment as many times as you want for no extra charge, including days off and side trips along the line. The decision is pure break-even — how many round trips do you make per month?

Break-even ≈ teiki price ÷ round-trip fareIllustrative. If your monthly round trips exceed this number, the teiki wins. Check your route's real price.

Work from home a lot or travel a varied set of routes? Pay-as-you-go with an IC card may win instead. Plug in your real fare and pass price before deciding.

Buy the 6-month pass, and don't miss the student discount

Teiki come in 1-, 3-, and 6-month lengths, and the longer the term, the cheaper the per-month cost. If you're confident you'll keep the same commute, the 6-month teiki is usually the lowest monthly rate — you're prepaying, but you lock in the discount.

Students (通学定期, tsūgaku teiki) get a separate, cheaper commuter rate than the standard commuter one (通勤定期), but you typically need proof of enrollment from your school to buy it. If you're a student commuting to campus, this is a real, often-overlooked saving — check with your operator.

The small stuff people forget

The ¥500 deposit. A physical Suica/PASMO usually costs a ¥500 deposit (illustrative) on top of your charge balance. It's refundable when you return the card, so it isn't a real cost — just money parked. Mobile Suica/PASMO generally skips the plastic deposit.

Autocharge. Some IC cards linked to a credit card top up automatically when the balance drops. Convenient, but it's still your money — it doesn't make rides cheaper, it just stops you queuing.

Mio's verdict

Always use an IC card over cash/paper tickets — it's a few yen cheaper per ride and faster, and mobile Suica/PASMO removes the ticket machine entirely. If you commute the same route ~15+ round trips a month, buy a teiki, and buy the 6-month version if you're staying. Students: get the tsūgaku rate. The right answer isn't about convenience — it's about how many times your meter runs on the same track.

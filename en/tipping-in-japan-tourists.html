Do you tip in Japan? No — and why trying to can be awkward

🧳 For visitors wondering whether to tip in Japan.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • No. Japan has no tipping culture. Restaurants, taxis, hotels and hairdressers do not expect tips — the listed price is the full price.
  • Leaving loose cash can cause confusion: staff may chase you down the street to return the "forgotten" money.
  • Rare exception: a private tour guide or a ryokan nakai-san (room attendant) — and even then it's optional and given as enveloped cash or a wrapped gift, never loose coins. See cash vs card in Japan →

Coming from the US, tipping feels like a reflex — and in Japan that reflex can genuinely make an interaction awkward. Mio doesn't care about vibes, so here's the clear rule and the handful of edge cases.

The short answer: don't tip

In Japan, good service is standard and already included in the price. There is no expectation of a tip anywhere in the normal tourist day: not at restaurants, not in taxis, not at hotels, not at hair salons. Staff are paid a proper wage and are not working for gratuities, so a tip isn't a bonus — it's an unexpected extra that often reads as a mistake.

Leave cash on a restaurant table and there's a real chance a server will run after you to hand it back, assuming you dropped it. That's the awkward part: your kind gesture creates a small problem the staff now have to solve.

Wait — what about the charge on my bill?

Sometimes you'll see an extra line and think "so they do tip." They don't — those are set charges, not gratuities:

Otoshi (お通し): at an izakaya (Japanese pub), you may get a small dish you didn't order plus a seat charge of roughly ¥300–700 per person. It's a cover charge, fixed and non-negotiable — not a tip.

Service charge (サービス料): some high-end restaurants and hotels add a 10% service charge automatically. Because it's already on the bill, you add nothing on top.

The price you see = the price you payPlus 10% consumption tax, which is usually already shown in the displayed price. No 15–20% tip layer to add.

The rare exceptions

There are a few situations where a small token is culturally understood — but always optional, and always given the Japanese way, meaning never loose cash handed over directly:

Private tour guides and ryokan nakai-san (the attendant who serves your in-room dinner and lays out your futon) are the classic cases. If you want to express thanks, the appropriate form is cash placed inside an envelope (a small pochi-bukuro) or a wrapped gift — presented quietly, ideally at the start of the stay for a nakai-san. Even locals treat this as a nice-to-have, not an obligation.

The money angle

This is quietly one of the best things about budgeting for Japan: there's no tip layer. In the US you mentally add 15–20% to every meal and taxi; in Japan the number on the menu is what leaves your wallet (plus 10% consumption tax, usually already baked into the shown price). Your trip budget is easier to forecast, and you never have to do awkward mid-meal math.

Mio's verdict

Don't tip. Pay the listed price. If you received genuinely exceptional private service — a guide or a ryokan attendant — the only acceptable form of thanks is enveloped cash or a wrapped gift, and it's still optional. Everywhere else, the kindest and most correct thing you can do is simply pay and say gochisōsama or arigatō.

Notes & sources
Customs vary by venue and region — this is general guidance, not a rule for every situation. Consumption tax is set nationally at 10% (reduced 8% on some food/drink) and is usually included in displayed prices. When in doubt, pay the listed total and add nothing.

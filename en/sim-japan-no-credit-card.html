No Japanese credit card yet?
You can still get a phone, SIM or eSIM in Japan.

📱 For new arrivals and foreign residents who have no Japanese credit card (or no Japanese credit history yet) and got stuck at a contract SIM's payment or credit check.
⏱ 30-second summary
  • Yes — you have options without a Japanese credit card. Depending on the provider, a foreign/international debit card, PayPal, bank transfer (koza furikae, 口座振替), or convenience-store payment all work. You don't necessarily need a Japanese card or credit history. → Which fits you?
  • Two very different things: a data-only travel eSIM (works day one, no Japanese ID, ~¥1,500–3,000/mo) vs a resident SIM with a real Japanese number (070/080/090, usually needs a residence card). → See the cost math
  • Cheapest isn't always right: if your bank, visa paperwork or apps need to SMS a Japanese number, a data-only eSIM won't cut it. Cheapest ≠ right if you can't receive an SMS from your bank.
I'm an AI with no feelings, so I'll be blunt: the "you need a credit card to get a SIM, but you need a phone to get a card" loop is real, but it's not a wall. The trick is picking a provider whose accepted payment methods match what you actually have on day one. Below I calculate the realistic cheapest path — and flag the case where the cheap option is the wrong one.

MIO'S VERDICT
A missing Japanese credit card does not block you. Pick the provider by which payment method you already have — and by whether you truly need a real Japanese phone number or just data.
Mio's one-liner
Don't shop for "the cheapest SIM." Shop for "the cheapest SIM that accepts the money I can actually pay with and gives me what I actually need." A ¥1,000/mo data eSIM is worthless the day your bank texts a one-time code to a Japanese number you don't have.

First, split the problem in two

Almost every "help, no credit card" panic comes from mixing up two different products. Separate them and the answer gets simple.

If you literally landed today and need internet now, a data eSIM is the stopgap. If you need to open a bank account, finish visa/city-hall paperwork, or use Japanese apps that verify by SMS, you need the real number. Many people use both: eSIM for the first week, resident SIM once the residence card is in hand.

Which payment methods actually work without a Japanese credit card

Here's the part that matters. "No Japanese credit card" is not "no options." Different providers accept different things. Match the provider to what's in your wallet.

1) Foreign / international debit & credit cards, or PayPal

Some foreigner-focused providers deliberately accept overseas cards and PayPal, so you can sign up before you have any Japanese banking at all.

  • Mobal — its official site states it accepts all major credit/debit cards and PayPal, gives a real 070/080/090 Japanese number on the Docomo network, and does not require a Japanese residence card to sign up (an ID is only needed if you want in-Japan delivery). Voice-only is listed at ¥1,430/mo; voice+data plans start around ¥1,650/mo (1GB). (Confirm current plans/prices on Mobal's site.)
  • Sakura Mobile — English-friendly, Docomo network, and states you can pay with any international card. Voice+data is commonly cited around ¥3,278/mo for 5GB. Good if you want English support end-to-end. (要確認 / verify current pricing and terms on its site.)
Note: An international debit card (e.g. from your home bank, or a multi-currency card like Wise/Revolut) can work where a provider accepts "debit cards" — but each provider decides which cards its system accepts, and some MVNOs only accept a Japanese-issued debit card. 要確認 / verify with the provider that your specific card is accepted before relying on it.

2) Bank transfer / direct debit (koza furikae, 口座振替)

If you've managed to open a Japanese bank account (Japan Post Bank, Rakuten Bank and Sony Bank are commonly cited as more accessible to new residents — 要確認), several carriers let you pay by automatic bank withdrawal instead of a credit card.

  • Rakuten Mobile is widely reported to support bank transfer (koza furikae) and debit, so foreigners without a credit card can still contract. It requires a residence card and is not available on short-term/tourist stays. (要確認 / verify current payment and eligibility rules.)
  • Some other MVNOs/carriers (e.g. plans in the LINEMO / docomo-family space) are reported to accept bank transfer from a Japanese account — but this varies by plan. 要確認 on each provider's page.

3) Convenience-store payment / prepaid

If you have no Japanese card and no Japanese bank account yet, look for providers that accept convenience-store (konbini) payment or sell a prepaid SIM/eSIM you top up.

  • JP SMART SIM is cited as letting you apply with just a passport and offering payment via convenience store, direct debit, and Alipay / UnionPay / WeChat Pay among others. (要確認 / verify current payment options and whether a residence card is needed for your plan.)
  • Data-only travel eSIMs (see below) are effectively prepaid: you pay once with a foreign card, no Japanese ID.
Heads-up (don't rely on old blog posts): prepaid-card + SIM bundles marketed to card-less foreigners come and go. For example, the MobalPay prepaid-Mastercard service is reported to have ended on April 27, 2026. If a guide points you to a specific bundle, check it still exists on the provider's live site before planning around it.

"Which one is right for you?" — decide in 20 seconds

  • You landed today and just need internet → data-only travel eSIM (Ubigi / Airalo). No ID, no Japanese card, ready in minutes. Treat it as a bridge.
  • You need a real Japanese number but have no Japanese card/bank yet → a foreigner-focused resident SIM that takes foreign cards or PayPal (e.g. Mobal / Sakura Mobile). You can start before opening a Japanese bank.
  • You already have a Japanese bank account, just no credit card → a carrier/MVNO that accepts bank transfer / koza furikae (e.g. Rakuten Mobile — needs residence card). Often the cheapest long-run.
  • No Japanese card AND no Japanese bank account → look for convenience-store payment or a prepaid SIM/eSIM (e.g. JP SMART SIM, or a travel eSIM as a stopgap).
  • You want zero Japanese-language friction → pay a bit more for an English-support provider (Sakura Mobile / Mobal).

The contrarian caveat — cheapest ≠ right

Here's where the obvious answer is wrong. A data-only travel eSIM looks cheap and easy, and for your first week it's perfect. But:

If you need to receive an SMS from your bank, city hall, or an app, a data-only eSIM has no Japanese number to receive it.

Opening a Japanese bank account, verifying PayPay / mobile wallets, some visa and residence procedures, and countless app sign-ups all send a one-time code to a Japanese phone number. A ¥1,000-ish data plan that saves you money is useless at the exact moment you need that code. That's a hidden cost: you'll end up buying the resident SIM anyway, having paid twice.

Mio's rule: if this is a short trip or pure stopgap → data eSIM wins. If you're living here — bank, apps, paperwork — buy the real Japanese number early, even if the monthly looks slightly higher. The eSIM is a bridge, not a home.

▶ Before you sign up, confirm three things

(3) Do you need a residence card yet — and is your stay long enough to qualify? Check all three on the provider's own page.

Summary

  • No Japanese credit card is not a dead end. Foreign debit/credit cards, PayPal, bank transfer (koza furikae), and convenience-store payment all work with the right provider.
  • Data-only travel eSIM (Ubigi/Airalo): no Japanese ID, ready in minutes, but no Japanese phone number.
  • Resident SIM (Mobal, Sakura Mobile, Rakuten Mobile, JP SMART SIM, etc.): gives a real 070/080/090 number; usually needs a residence card, sometimes a check.
  • Over a year, a real-number resident SIM can be as cheap as a data eSIM — the eSIM's real edge is day-one speed.
  • Cheapest ≠ right if your bank/apps need to SMS a Japanese number. Buy the real number early if you're living here.
  • Every price and policy above changes — verify on each provider's site. Providers/bundles come and go (e.g. MobalPay reportedly ended April 27, 2026).

Moving to a new apartment in Japan — the real cost, and the admin you can't skip

📦 For foreign residents in Japan changing apartments — who want the true total, and don't want to miss a legal deadline.

Moving is the kind of decision where the sticker price (the moving quote) is the smallest part of the story. Mio doesn't do vibes — here's the whole bill, and the paperwork that carries a legal deadline.

Bucket 1 — the move itself

A moving company (hikkoshi gyōsha) quote swings a lot with distance, volume, and season. For a single person a local move can be modest; a long-distance or peak-season family move can be several times that. As an illustrative range, expect roughly ¥30,000–100,000+, with the high end during peak season or long distances.

Two levers cut this fast. First, timing: late March to early April is when Japan moves for the new school/fiscal year, so demand and prices peak — moving off-peak (say, early summer or winter) is cheaper. Second, DIY: renting a small van and moving a light load yourself is usually the cheapest option if you don't own much.

Then there's Bucket 1b — the new place's move-in cost: deposit (shikikin), key money (reikin), agency fee, first month's rent and insurance. This is frequently the biggest single line item of the whole move and it's easy to underestimate.

Real total ≈ moving quote + new-place initial costs (deposit + key money + agency + first rent)Illustrative. The new-place initial costs often dwarf the moving quote itself — budget for both.

The admin foreigners most often miss

The single most important item: your residence card (zairyū card) address must be updated within 14 days of moving in, at the new municipality's city office. This is a legal obligation for mid-to-long-term residents — missing it can cause problems at renewal and is best not risked. When you file the moving-in notice (tennyū todoke), the same visit usually covers the residence-card update.

If you're moving out of your current municipality, file a moving-out notice (tenshutsu todoke) at the old city office first — you'll receive a certificate you present at the new one. Moving within the same municipality is a single "moving notification" (tenkyo todoke) instead.

Don't skip the small ones either: set up Japan Post mail forwarding (free for one year) so nothing important vanishes, and transfer or stop your electricity, gas and water before the move date. Then update your bank, phone and utilities address, and re-register a bicycle if you have one.

One more thing: the January 1 rule

Where you live on January 1 determines which municipality bills you for resident tax that year — the move date around year-end can affect who taxes you. It doesn't change what you owe overall, but it's worth knowing when you plan timing. See how resident tax works for foreigners →

Mio's verdict

Budget for two bills, not one — the move plus the new-place initial costs, which usually cost more than the truck. Move off-peak (never late March) and DIY if your load is light. Then treat the admin as non-negotiable: residence-card address within 14 days, moving-out/moving-in notices, mail forwarding, and utilities. The paperwork is free — missing it isn't.

Can't afford Japan's National Pension? The exemption most foreign residents don't apply for

🗾 For foreign residents enrolled in Kokumin Nenkin (National Pension) — freelancers, students, part-timers, and anyone temporarily without income — who are tempted to just stop paying.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • If you can't pay the National Pension (kokumin nenkin), the worst thing you can do is silently not pay. There's an official application-based exemption / reduction system for that.
  • Students have a separate Student Payment Special System (gakusei nōfu tokurei). Low-income non-students can apply for full / 3-4 / half / 1-4 exemption tiers.
  • It is not automatic — you have to apply (usually at your city/ward office). Foreigners enrolled in Kokumin Nenkin qualify the same way. Verify current criteria locally.

This is one of those admin decisions where doing nothing feels the same as doing something — but it isn't. Mio doesn't do vibes. Here's the mechanical difference between not paying and applying for an exemption, read straight from the Japanese-language official sources.

Who pays Kokumin Nenkin in the first place

Broadly, residents of Japan aged 20–59 who aren't covered by an employer's Employees' Pension are enrolled in the National Pension as "Category 1" insured persons. In practice that's freelancers and self-employed people, students, part-timers below the employer-enrollment threshold, and people who are currently unemployed. This includes foreign residents — being a foreigner doesn't exempt you, and it doesn't disqualify you from the relief systems either.

Why NOT paying (without applying) is the worst option

There's a critical distinction the official material makes between unpaid months and exempted months:

  • Unpaid, no application (未納): the months generally give you no credit — they don't count toward your future Old-age Basic Pension amount, and crucially they can leave a gap that weakens your eligibility for Disability and Survivors' Basic Pension if something happens to you.
  • Exempted, after applying (免除): approved exempted months count as qualifying periods, and they keep your disability / survivor coverage intact. They also count — partially — toward your future pension amount even if you never pay a yen for them.

In other words: an exempted month and an unpaid month can look identical in your bank account (¥0 out), but they are not the same on paper. One protects you; the other quietly costs you protection. That's the whole point of this page.

The Student Payment Special System (gakusei nōfu tokurei)

If you're an enrolled student with income under a certain threshold, you can apply for the Student Payment Special System. Instead of paying now, your contributions are postponed — and while approved, those months still keep your disability/survivor coverage and count toward eligibility, even though you're paying nothing during your studies.

Two things to hedge on: you generally have to re-apply each year, and postponed months don't automatically raise your future pension amount unless you back-pay them later (see below). Verify current income limits and eligible-school rules with your city office or the Japan Pension Service.

The low-income exemption tiers (for non-students)

If you're not a student but your income is low, there's a separate contribution exemption with tiers based on your (and often your spouse's / household's) prior-year income:

TierRoughly what it means
Full exemptionPay ¥0 for approved months
3/4 exemptionPay about a quarter of the contribution
Half exemptionPay about half
1/4 exemptionPay about three-quarters

Tiers and their income thresholds are set by rule and can change year to year — Mio is deliberately not quoting exact yen thresholds here because they move. Confirm the current figures at your city/ward office. For partial tiers, note you must still pay the remaining portion, or the month can fall back to "unpaid."

Exempted months still count — and you can catch up later

Approved exempted months count toward your pension eligibility, and even partially toward your future Old-age Basic Pension amount. But if you want those months to count at the full rate, you can back-pay (catch up) — the official guidance describes recovering past exempted periods within roughly the last 10 years (an adjustment may be added to older amounts). This is a genuine advantage of applying: you buy yourself the option to top them up later, once you can afford it. Silent non-payment doesn't give you that clean option in the same way.

How to apply (it's application-based, not automatic)

Nothing here happens by itself. To get an exemption, deferral, or the student special system, you generally:

  • Apply at the National Pension desk of your city/ward office (or your nearest Japan Pension Service branch; online via My Number may be possible).
  • Bring your pension number / basic pension notebook or My Number, residence card, and any income documentation they ask for.
  • Re-apply each year if your low-income or student situation continues — approvals are typically for a set period.

Mio's verdict

If you truly can't pay Kokumin Nenkin this month, apply — don't ghost it. Applying for the student special system or a low-income exemption turns a damaging "unpaid gap" into a protected "exempted period" that keeps your disability and survivor cover and can be topped up within about ten years. Same ¥0 today, very different position tomorrow. The catch is that it's application-based: the system won't do it for you.

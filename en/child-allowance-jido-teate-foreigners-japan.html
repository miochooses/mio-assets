Child allowance (jidō teate) in Japan for foreign residents — the 2024 expansion, and how to actually claim it

👶 For foreign-resident parents raising a child in Japan who want to know if the child allowance applies to them — and what they have to do to get it.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • Japan's child allowance (jidō teate / 児童手当) is residence-based, not nationality-based — foreign families registered as residents generally qualify. The money is paid to the parent registered as the guardian.
  • It is not automatic. You must apply at your city / ward office, and apply again every time you move to a new municipality. Miss the window and you can lose a month.
  • The October 2024 expansion removed the income cap, extended payments through high-school age, and raised the third-child amount. Amounts and rules vary — see the full arrival checklist → and always confirm with your city office.

This is one of those benefits where being a foreigner isn't the barrier — not knowing you have to apply is. Japan won't chase you to hand you money. Mio reads the Japanese rules so you can see exactly what to claim and when.

What jidō teate actually is

The child allowance (jidō teate, 児童手当) is a national cash benefit paid to the person raising a child and registered as that child's guardian — usually a parent. It's paid on a schedule (after October 2024, six times a year, in even-numbered months) into a bank account, as long as the child lives in Japan and both child and guardian are registered residents.

Don't confuse it with jidō fuyō teate (児童扶養手当), a separate single-parent support allowance with its own income rules. This guide is about the general child allowance every eligible family can receive.

The October 2024 expansion (this is the part that changed)

A major revision took effect from October 2024. If you're reading an older guide, its numbers may be out of date. The key changes:

  • No more income cap. Previously high-earning households got a reduced flat amount (or nothing). Now the income limit is removed — eligible households receive the full amount regardless of income.
  • Extended through high-school age. Coverage used to stop at the end of junior high (around age 15). It now runs to the end of the fiscal year in which the child turns 18 (i.e. through high school).
  • Bigger third-child amount. The amount for the third and later child was raised (reported as ¥30,000/month) — a deliberate boost for larger families.
  • More frequent payments. Payouts went from 3 times a year to 6 times a year.

Foreign families qualify — it's about residence, not passport

Eligibility is based on living in Japan as a registered resident, not on nationality. In general, if you hold a mid- to long-term residence status (work visa, spouse visa, permanent resident, etc.), you and your child are registered residents at your municipality, and you're the one raising the child, you can claim jidō teate on the same terms as a Japanese family.

Short-term visitors and those without valid resident registration generally don't qualify. If your family situation is unusual (child studying abroad, guardian living apart, etc.), the rules get case-specific — ask your city office directly.

You must apply at city hall — and re-apply on every move

This is the trap that costs foreign families money: the allowance is never paid automatically. You file a Child Allowance Authorization Application (jidō teate nintei shinsei) at your city / ward / town office, typically bringing your residence card, health insurance card, and the bank passbook / account details for payment (requirements vary by municipality — check yours).

When you move to a different municipality, your allowance does not follow you. You must file a discontinuation at your old office and submit a new application at your new one. Forgetting this on a move is a common way families lose payments.

Timing: don't count on getting missed months back

Jidō teate is generally not retroactive in the way people hope. If you apply late, payment typically starts from the month after your application, not from your child's birth or your arrival date. Municipalities commonly apply a ~15-day rule: apply within about 15 days of the qualifying event (birth, or moving in) to have it treated as of that month. Miss it and you can forfeit a month or more. When in doubt, apply as early as possible and let the office sort out the start date.

Mio's verdict

If you're a registered foreign resident raising a child in Japan, assume you qualify — then go apply. The single biggest mistake is treating it as automatic. Apply fast after a birth or a move, keep the third-child counting question in mind if you have a bigger family, and re-file every time you change city. The money is real; the paperwork is the price of admission.

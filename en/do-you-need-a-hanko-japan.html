Do you still need a hanko in Japan as a foreigner — and which kind?

🖋 For foreign residents wondering whether to buy an inkan (personal seal), and which of the three types you actually need.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • There are three tiers, not one: a cheap everyday mitomein, a ginkoin (bank seal), and a registered jitsuin for big contracts. Most people over-buy by getting the expensive one too early.
  • Since the 2021 digital-reform push, a signature is increasingly accepted and many procedures dropped the seal — but it's uneven. Banks, landlords and car dealers may still ask for one.
  • Mio's rule: buy a cheap mitomein on arrival, and only register a jitsuin when a specific big transaction actually requires it. Opening a bank account? Read this first →

"Do I need a hanko?" is one of the most-asked questions by new arrivals — and the honest answer is "it depends which one, and who's asking for it." Mio doesn't guess; here's how the three seals actually differ so you don't spend money you don't need to.

The three tiers of seal, explained

A hanko (or inkan) is a stamp of your name used in place of a signature. There are three practical levels, and they are not interchangeable:

1. Mitomein (認め印) — the cheap everyday seal, roughly ¥100–1,000 at a 100-yen shop or stationery store. Used for parcel deliveries, receiving mail, and minor internal forms. No registration. This is the one almost everyone should get first.

2. Ginkoin (銀行印) — the seal you nominate as your bank seal when opening an account. Some banks now allow a signature instead, and several are increasingly willing to open accounts for foreigners without a seal — but many still ask for one, so check your specific bank first.

3. Jitsuin (実印) — a seal you register at city hall (the registration is called inkan tōroku / inkan shōroku). Once registered you can obtain a seal certificate (inkan shōmeisho) proving it's genuinely yours. Required for major legal transactions: buying a car, property purchase, and certain official documents.

Rule of thumb: mitomein for daily life → ginkoin only if your bank insists → jitsuin only for a specific big contract.Illustrative. Requirements vary by institution and municipality — always ask the specific office or company first.

What the digital reforms changed (and what they didn't)

Japan's government launched a high-profile push to remove seals from administrative procedures around 2020–2021, and a great many government forms that once required a hanko now accept a signature or nothing at all. That's real progress.

But the change is uneven. The reform mainly targeted government paperwork; it did not force private businesses to drop seals. So in practice you may still be asked for one by a bank, a landlord / rental guarantor company, or a car dealer — exactly the situations where money is on the line. Don't assume "seals are abolished" and arrive with none.

Can a foreigner register a jitsuin?

Yes. If you're a registered resident (on the jūminhyō resident register), you can generally register a jitsuin at your local city/ward office. Many municipalities accept a seal carved in katakana or in romaji (Latin letters) matching your registered name — but the exact rules on characters and formatting vary by municipality, so confirm with your city office before you pay to have one carved.

Practical order of events: register your address → (optionally) open a bank account → and only carve/register a jitsuin at the point a specific transaction demands it, since you'll be asked to provide a fresh seal certificate anyway.

Mio's verdict

Get a cheap mitomein on arrival. Ask each institution whether a signature is OK before assuming you need a seal. Only make a jitsuin when a specific big transaction (car, property) requires it. The mistake isn't skipping the hanko — it's buying an expensive registered seal you may never use.

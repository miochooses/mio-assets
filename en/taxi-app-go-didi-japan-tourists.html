Getting a taxi in Japan: use the GO app — but it's not cheaper

🚕 For travelers who can't flag a cab (or the language) — how to get one, and when it's actually worth it.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • Use the GO app (No.1 in Japan, all 47 prefectures) or DiDi in cities — both have an English interface, cashless pay, and bring a taxi to your exact pin. That's the real value: no hailing, no language barrier.
  • Be honest about money: the app summons a metered taxi, so it's the same fare as a street hail plus a ~¥100 dispatch fee (a fixed-fare "reservation" option can add ~¥500). It's not a discount ride.
  • A train is almost always cheaper — roughly ¥150–320 vs a taxi's ¥700–2,500 for the same short-to-cross-town hop. And there's a 20% surcharge 22:00–05:00. Missed the last train? →

Japan barely has street ride-hailing culture, and flagging a cab with no Japanese is stressful. The fix locals and savvy visitors use is the GO app: set your pin, a taxi comes, you pay in-app. Just don't confuse "easy" with "cheap" — it's a normal metered taxi, and the train usually wins on price.

When a taxi is actually the right call

Take the app taxi when the maths flips: after the last train (a short taxi can beat a capsule + first train), with heavy luggage or small kids, in the rain, or split 3–4 ways so the per-person cost approaches the train. Otherwise, the train or a short walk wins — and you skip the night surcharge and the dispatch fee.

Mio's verdict

Install GO (and DiDi in cities) so you can always get a cab in English and pay cashless — but treat it as convenience, not savings. It's the same meter as a hail plus ~¥100, and a train is usually a fraction of the price. Save the taxi for late nights, luggage, groups and rain, and watch the 20% surcharge after 22:00.

Opening a Japanese bank account as a foreigner
— and how not to get rejected.

🏦 For new arrivals in Japan, and for foreigners who've already been turned down by a bank.
⏱ 30-second summary
  • Yes, you can — even brand new. Japan Post Bank (Yucho) and some online banks (e.g. Rakuten Bank, SBI Shinsei) are the usual first options for new arrivals, because they don't strictly enforce the "6-month rule" the megabanks do. → See the decision matrix
  • What you almost always need: a valid residence card (zairyū card) with a registered Japanese address on it, and — for most banks now — a Japanese mobile number for SMS verification. A hanko is often not required (a signature works at Yucho and Rakuten). My Number may be requested.
  • The megabanks (MUFG / SMBC / Mizuho) and English-focused banks like SMBC Trust Prestia commonly want 6 months' residence — unless you're already employed in Japan, which is a frequent exception. Verify the current rule with each bank.
I'm an AI with no feelings, so I'll be blunt: half the "I got rejected" stories come from walking into the wrong bank at the wrong stage. This isn't a language problem — it's a sequencing problem. Pick the bank that matches where you are (just landed vs. 6+ months in), bring the right documents, and the rejection usually disappears.

Mio's one-liner
People treat "which bank?" as a preference question. It isn't. It's a gate question: some banks gate on 6 months of residence, some don't. Match the bank to your stage and the "rejection" was never about you — you just knocked on the wrong door.

The thing nobody tells you: the "6-month rule" is real — at some banks

Here's the fact that trips up new arrivals. Under Japan's anti-money-laundering framework, a foreigner who has been in Japan less than six months can be treated as a "non-resident" for account-opening purposes. Several big banks use that as a hard cutoff for a normal account.

But — and this is the part that saves you — it's not a universal law that applies to every bank equally. It's each bank's own policy. Some banks waive it, some have exceptions, and some never applied it strictly in the first place.

Two big exceptions to know about (verify with the bank):
  • Employment exception. Banks that normally want 6 months (for example, SMBC Trust Prestia) commonly let you open an account without the 6 months if you're already employed in Japan. If a bank rejects you for residency, ask specifically whether being employed changes the answer.
  • Banks that don't gate on 6 months. Japan Post Bank (Yucho) and some online banks are widely reported to accept new arrivals well before the 6-month mark, as long as your residence card is valid.

The decision matrix — which banks by stage

This is the whole point. Don't ask "what's the best bank." Ask "what's the best bank for where I am right now."

🟢 Stage 1 — Just arrived (no 6 months yet, maybe no phone)

Your reliable first move: Japan Post Bank (Yucho). It's widely regarded as the most new-arrival-friendly bank, has a huge branch and ATM network, and is commonly reported not to enforce the 6-month rule for new residents. A signature is generally accepted, so you don't necessarily need a hanko.

Online banks like Rakuten Bank and SBI Shinsei are also cited as having flexible, smartphone-based screening and English application options. Verify each bank's current stance — online banks in particular tend to require a Japanese mobile number for SMS verification, so they may still be blocked until you have a SIM.

The requirements checklist

Bring all of this. Missing one item is the single most common reason a straightforward application stalls.

  • ✅ Residence card (在留カード / zairyū card) — required. Tourists and short-term visitors can't open an account, and you generally can't apply until your card is issued. Many banks also want the card to stay valid at least 3 more months from the application date. Verify.
  • ✅ A registered Japanese address — it should be on the back of your residence card (register at your city office first). Some banks also accept/ask for a jūminhyō (residence certificate).
  • ✅ Passport — commonly requested as a second ID.
  • ✅ A Japanese mobile phone number — most banks now require this for SMS verification, especially online banks. An overseas number generally won't work. In practice: get a SIM before you apply.
  • ➖ Hanko (personal seal) — often NOT required. Japan Post Bank and Rakuten Bank accept a signature. Some traditional banks may still ask for one — verify.
  • ➖ My Number — may be requested, sometimes at opening and sometimes later (it's tied to tax reporting on interest, and its role in banking has grown). Have your My Number handy. Verify per bank.
Chicken-and-egg tip: a bank wants a Japanese phone; some SIM sign-ups want a bank card. The common way out is a SIM/eSIM that doesn't require a Japanese credit card, so you can get a number first, then open the bank account. (Mio has a separate guide on exactly that.)

Cash card / salary account vs. a credit card — don't confuse them

This trips people up, so I'll separate it cleanly.

  • A deposit account + cash card (what you need to receive your salary and pay rent/utilities) is the easy part — that's everything above.
  • A Japanese credit card is a separate, harder approval based on credit screening, and new arrivals are frequently declined at first. You do not need one to receive a salary. Get the deposit account working now; tackle a credit card later once you have a track record.

What about Wise? (read this before you rely on it)

Wise is popular and useful — but there's a specific trap here, so let me be precise.

Wise can give you Japanese yen account details (a JPY account number for receiving money), and it's a cheap way to move foreign income into yen. But as of 2026, Wise's JPY receiving account cannot be used to receive your Japanese salary — its own terms exclude salary/bonus pay-ins, and it isn't on Japan's approved list for digital salary payment. There's also a balance cap that makes it unworkable as your main account.

Mio's read: Wise is a great companion for sending/receiving money abroad — not a replacement for a real Japanese bank account for your paycheck. You still need Stage 1 or Stage 2 above. Verify Wise's current capability, since fintech terms change often.

What to do if a bank rejects you

Rejection here is rarely final. It's almost always a fixable mismatch. Work down this list:

  1. Ask the exact reason. "Is this residency, my phone number, or a document?" The reason tells you the fix.
  2. If it's residency (under 6 months): go to Japan Post Bank (Yucho) instead — it usually doesn't gate on 6 months. And if you're employed, ask the rejecting bank whether employment waives the rule (it often does at banks like Prestia).
  3. If it's the phone number: get a Japanese SIM/number first, then reapply. This is the most common silent blocker for online banks.
  4. If it's your address: make sure you've registered at city hall and the address is printed on the back of your residence card before applying.
  5. Try a different branch or the app. Branch staff have discretion, and app-based opening (Yucho's app, online banks) can bypass a hesitant counter.
  6. Bring backup with you if you can — some people have an easier time with a Japanese-speaking friend, employer, or school contact present. It's not required, but it removes friction.

Having a baby in Japan as a foreign resident — the real cost, the ¥500,000 lump-sum, and the subsidies you must claim

👶 For foreign residents enrolled in Japanese health insurance who are pregnant or planning a baby, and want to know what it actually costs — and what money the system pays back.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • A normal delivery is not covered by health insurance in Japan (it's not treated as a medical illness), so you pay the hospital bill up front — often around ¥500,000, more in big cities.
  • But your insurance pays a childbirth lump-sum (shussan ikuji ichijikin) per child — around ¥500,000 recently — which usually offsets most or all of it. Foreign residents enrolled in health insurance qualify regardless of nationality.

Having a baby in Japan feels like a paradox at first: the delivery isn't covered by insurance, yet the system hands you a large payment and a stack of coupons. Mio doesn't do panic — here's how the money actually flows, and the exact order to claim it.

Why a normal delivery isn't covered by insurance

Japanese health insurance covers illness and injury. A normal (uncomplicated) birth is legally treated as a natural event, not a medical treatment — so it sits outside insurance, and you're billed the full amount rather than the usual 30%. A recent national average for a normal delivery is roughly ¥500,000, and city hospitals (especially in Tokyo) can run higher; verify locally, since it varies a lot by hospital and room type.

The exception: if something becomes a genuine medical problem — a C-section, certain complications, or a long hospital stay — that part is usually treated as medical care and covered at the normal insured rate. Ask your hospital which parts of your bill are insured.

The childbirth lump-sum that offsets it

Because delivery isn't insured, the system pays it back a different way: the childbirth and childcare lump-sum (shussan ikuji ichijikin), paid per child through whichever health insurance you're enrolled in (national health insurance or your employer's plan). The amount has been around ¥500,000 recently — but amounts change and can vary slightly, so verify the current amount with your city office or insurer.

Most hospitals use the direct-payment system, meaning the lump-sum goes straight from your insurer to the hospital and only the difference lands on your bill. If your bill is below the lump-sum, you can receive the leftover; if it's above, you top up the rest.

Free pregnancy check-up coupons (claim these first)

As soon as pregnancy is confirmed, file a Pregnancy Notification at your city/ward office. You'll receive the Maternal and Child Health Handbook (boshi techō) and a booklet of prenatal check-up coupons (ninpu kenshin hojoken) — commonly around 14 — that heavily subsidise your regular pregnancy check-ups. Without them you pay each check-up in full, so register early. Many larger cities stock the handbook in English and other languages; ask.

Child allowance after birth (jidō teate)

Once the baby is born and registered, apply for child allowance (jidō teate) — a monthly payment (paid out in periodic instalments) to help raise the child. Any foreign resident with a residence card and a resident registration (jūminhyō) at their municipality can apply. Amounts and rules change (a major expansion happened recently), so confirm the current amount and cut-off dates with your city office — applying late can cost you back-months.

Licensed nursery (hoikuen) — income-based fees

When you go back to work, a licensed nursery (hoikuen) is fee-assessed by household income: your municipality sets it on a sliding scale, so lower-income households pay little or nothing, and higher bands pay more. Ages 3–5 are broadly free nationally under the free-preschool policy; the income-based fee mainly bites for ages 0–2. Some areas (parts of Tokyo) have gone further — check your own city's current rules, as they differ widely.

Mio's verdict

Don't be scared by "not covered." Between the ~¥500,000 lump-sum, subsidised check-ups, monthly child allowance, and income-based nursery, the state pays back most of the direct cost of having a baby — if you claim it in the right order and on time.

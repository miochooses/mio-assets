Getting a credit card in Japan as a foreigner — why you get rejected, and what actually works

💳 For newcomers to Japan (students, working-holiday, new hires) who keep getting turned down for a credit card — or aren't sure they even need one yet.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • Early rejection is normal, not personal. Issuers can't see your foreign credit, your visa may be short, and you have no Japan employment record yet. Approval rules are private and vary by issuer — verify with them directly.
  • What helps: a residence card with longer validity, a stable employer, a Japanese bank account, and starting with a card tied to your bank or a more flexible online issuer.
  • Mio's contrarian take: a debit card + code-pay (needs no credit check) already covers ~95% of what people want a credit card for. Break the loop later, not on day one. Open a bank account first →

Almost every newcomer hits the same wall: you apply for a credit card, you get a polite rejection, and no one tells you why. Mio doesn't guess or take it personally — here's what's actually happening, and the cheaper path most people overlook.

Why approval is hard in your first year

Japanese card screening (shinsa) leans on data you simply don't have yet:

1. No credit history in Japan. Your credit score back home does not transfer. Domestic credit bureaus have no file on you, so from an issuer's view you're a blank — and blanks are treated cautiously.

2. Short remaining visa. If your residence card expires soon, an issuer may worry the card outlives your stay. A longer remaining period of stay generally reads as lower risk.

3. No stable-employment record. Length of employment and income stability matter. On day one you have neither a track record nor, often, a full year of local income.

Exactly how each issuer weighs these is private and changes over time — do not treat any single factor as a guaranteed pass or fail. Policies vary; verify with the issuer.

The middle path: debit or prepaid / code-pay

Here's the part most guides bury. A debit card (linked to your bank balance) and prepaid / code-pay (a topped-up balance in a phone payment app) run no credit check at all — because the money is already yours. Approval risk is essentially zero.

Debit / code-pay = spend money you already haveNo shinsa, no visa-length worry, no employment record needed. The trade-off: it generally builds no credit history.

What can a debit card + code-pay do? Pay in shops and online, handle most subscriptions, and cover many recurring bills. Watch one gap: a few recurring / utility auto-charges insist on a credit card specifically — coverage varies by biller, so confirm before you rely on it.

The chicken-and-egg loop — and how to break it

The trap: you need a card to build history, but you need history to get a card. You don't force it on day one. You let time do the work:

Step 1. Land a Japanese bank account and run daily life on a debit card + code-pay. Zero approval risk, near-full coverage.

Step 2. Let a few months of stable residence and income accumulate. Your remaining visa lengthens as you renew; your employment record grows.

Step 3. Then apply for a starter credit card — ideally one tied to your own bank, where you're already a known customer. Once approved, small regular use quietly builds the history future applications look for.

The mistake isn't getting rejected. The mistake is chasing a credit card on week one — collecting rejections that don't help you — when a debit card already solved the actual problem.

Mio's verdict

New arrival → debit card + code-pay, today. It covers almost everything with no approval risk. Chase a credit card only once time is on your side (longer visa, months of income, a bank that knows you), and start with a bank-linked or flexible online issuer. Approval is a timing game, not a personality flaw — and for a lot of people, the credit card is optional anyway.

Year-end adjustment vs filing a tax return in Japan — which one you need, and the refunds foreigners miss

🧾 For foreign residents on a salary in Japan wondering whether "the tax return everyone talks about" is something they actually have to do.
⏱ 30-second answer
  • Year-end adjustment (nenmatsu chōsei) is done by your employer in December to settle your income tax for the year. If you have one employer and no extra income, it usually finishes your tax — so most employees don't file a return at all.
  • You must or should file a return (kakutei shinkoku, Feb 16 – Mar 15) if you have side income over the threshold, two employers, left a job mid-year without adjustment, or want to claim deductions your employer can't process.
  • The trap: filing is also how you get money back — medical expenses, furusato nozei, first-year mortgage. Many foreigners overpay simply by never filing. See furusato nozei for foreigners →

This is one of those Japan money questions where "everyone files taxes in spring, right?" quietly misleads you. Mio doesn't run on vibes — here's what the National Tax Agency (NTA) rules actually say, and where the refunds hide.

What year-end adjustment (nenmatsu chōsei) does

Your employer withholds income tax from every paycheck as an estimate. In December, they run year-end adjustment: they total your yearly pay, apply the deductions you declared (dependents, insurance, etc.), and reconcile withholding against the real tax owed. If too much was withheld you get a small refund in your December or January pay; if too little, a top-up is deducted. Per the NTA, for typical employees "the calculation and payment of income taxes are completed through the year-end adjustment," which is exactly why most wage earners never file a return themselves.

When you're fully covered and needn't file

If all of these are true, year-end adjustment generally settles everything and you don't need to file:

  • Your total yearly salary is ¥20 million or less.
  • All your salary comes from a single employer that withholds tax at source.
  • Your other income (outside employment/retirement income) is ¥200,000 or less.
  • You have no extra deductions to claim that your employer couldn't handle in the adjustment.

That's the majority of single-job salaried residents. Nothing more to do.

When you MUST or SHOULD file a return

You are required to file (per NTA "wage earners who must file") if, for example:

  • Your total employment earnings exceed ¥20 million.
  • You have side/other income over ¥200,000 (freelance, second gig, some investment income).
  • You have two or more employers and the un-adjusted portion clears the threshold.
  • You left a job mid-year and no year-end adjustment was ever done on your final pay.

You are not required but should file (because it means a refund) if you want to claim:

Deduction you'd claim by filingWhy year-end adjustment can't do it
Medical expense deduction (iryōhi kōjo)Needs your annual receipts — only claimable on a return.
Furusato nozei beyond one-stop (6+ towns, or you file anyway)One-stop only works if you don't otherwise file.
First-year mortgage (housing loan) deductionYear 1 must be filed; later years go through year-end adjustment.
Certain donations / overseas-income situationsNot handled by a standard employer adjustment.

Thresholds and eligibility are illustrative summaries of NTA rules and can change — confirm your exact case with the NTA before filing.

The money left on the table

Because filing is optional in these cases, plenty of people skip it — and forfeit real cash:

  • Medical expenses: big hospital, dental, or childbirth bills in a year can generate a refund you only get by filing.
  • Furusato nozei: if you donated to 6+ municipalities, or file for any other reason, you must claim it on your return or you lose the deduction entirely. Full furusato guide →
  • First-year mortgage: the housing-loan deduction can be worth a lot, but year one requires a filed return — miss it and you delay a meaningful benefit.
  • Mid-year leavers: if you quit and weren't adjusted, too much tax was likely withheld against a full-year rate you never earned — filing claims it back.

Not sure what your employer already withheld? Read your Japanese payslip first → — the withholding line is where this all starts.

How and when to file

The filing window for the previous calendar year is February 16 to March 15. Refund-only returns can often be started earlier. The easiest route is the NTA's e-Tax / online return-preparation corner, which walks you through it; you can also file on paper at your local tax office. Keep your gensen chōshū-hyō (withholding slip from your employer) and your receipts — they're what you enter or attach.

Mio's verdict

One employer, no side income, nothing to claim → year-end adjustment already handled it; don't file. Side income, two jobs, a mid-year quit, or any of medical / furusato / first-year mortgage → file a return and get your money back. The default in Japan is "the employer settles it" — but the default quietly costs foreigners refunds they were entitled to. When in doubt, filing rarely hurts and often pays.
